The second-language myth

Speaking a second language fluently is a big dream for many people, and especially for a lot of us Brazilians. I dare say most of us think of English first, mainly because it is practically the most used language between different countries around the world, and also because it is one of the easiest to learn.

Here in Brazil, however, a second language is not really necessary: Portuguese covers almost everything we need, and foreigners who live here end up speaking Portuguese, even though it is not one of the easiest languages to learn.

According to the newspaper O Globo, only 5% of Brazilians are considered fluent in English, for example. In fact, very few Brazilians actually need a second language for a real purpose, whether professional or personal.

Naturally, the most common option while we stay in Brazil is the famous language school, which offers two or three hours of class a week and usually charges quite a lot for it.

The question is: are those classes enough to make someone fluent in a language?

I can't give an exact answer, but in my opinion they are not, mostly because it takes an enormous amount of practice for someone to develop another language.

Classes do help you find your way and give you the basics, so you learn the theory and become able to practice the language in other situations. Still, I think going to class isn't everything. I'll say more about that in item 5 of the list below.

With that in mind, here are a few tips based on my own experience with my second language.

1. Get out of your comfort zone

When we can choose between our native language and the second one, talking in our native language is far more pleasant and comfortable. But it isn't your native language that needs practice. It's the extra one.

Note that imagining that choice is different from actually living it. In your head it's easy to pick English, but the moment you feel stuck because you can't express your idea, if the other person understands Portuguese you will have an overwhelming urge to carry on in Portuguese. That is, assuming a Brazilian learning English.

The tip is: stay in the discomfort and keep the conversation in the other language!

Also, switch all your devices to the new language: phone, computer, tablet and so on. It's a bit annoying at first, but little by little you learn to deal with the new words.

I have a blast when my GPS starts giving me directions in Spanish, for example. And as a bonus, it trains your ear a little.

Watch videos online with subtitles in the same language (for example, English audio with English subtitles) or, better yet, with no subtitles at all! That way you're forced to pay attention to what people are saying in the movie, series or documentary.

Try reading things in the language: blogs, books, any kind of text. In my opinion this is actually the best way to start, because reading is one of the easiest skills to acquire.

2. Avoid people who correct you all the time

You know that person who keeps correcting you while you talk to them in your extra language? Even though they know you're learning, that you're making an effort and maybe even that you're studying, they insist on interrupting and correcting you all the time, when all you want is to get your message across.

The tip is: run away from that person, they're really annoying!

Communication happens when you can get your message across to one or more people and when you can understand them. That's all.

Often the person understands you, knows what you mean and still insists on correcting you, even after noticing that you find it really irritating.

3. Remember that annoying person? It's very important that you don't become one

Come on, no hypocrisy here.

If someone is talking to you in their second language and you get the message, ignore the pronunciation and grammar mistakes. If they want feedback on how they use the language, they'll ask you.

4. The rule is: speak, speak a lot, speak as much as you can, whether you're right or wrong

The truth is that we can notice most of our own mistakes right after we say something wrong in a language we're learning.

Another fact is that more outgoing people get faster results when learning a new language, precisely because they aren't very embarrassed to say something wrong. So they end up practicing a lot and improving faster than shyer, more reserved people.

And if you consider yourself shy or reserved, don't think a new language is out of your reach. Everyone has their own learning curve: some learn faster, others more slowly. The point is that all of us can learn if we respect our own limits and, ideally, fight our comfort zone and break our own records.

In short, just like any other skill you'd like to acquire, the rule is no different for a new language. You need to practice, and practice a lot!

And when I say speak a lot, I don't mean giving someone a monologue, but having a balanced conversation where you both speak and listen a lot.

5. If you can, go live in a country whose native language is the one you want to learn

Let's think about some very rough numbers.

If you take two one-hour language classes a week, that's about 8 hours of practice a month and 96 hours a year.

If you live in a country where you have no choice but to use the local language, let's conservatively say you use it 8 hours a day. In just 12 days you practice as much as you would in a whole year of English classes. See the difference?

It's worth stressing that language classes are worthwhile! My point is that, on their own, they aren't enough. You need to go beyond the classroom to really develop the skills of a new language.

Unfortunately, not everyone has the chance or the money to study abroad. So what then? What do I do?

Remember that we have the internet now. You can sign up for big social networks (such as Duolingo, Busuu, My Happy Planet, etc.) and talk to people all over the world with the same goal as yours: practicing a language, whichever it is!

You can also host people from different countries at home through Couchsurfing or even Airbnb. These are amazing ways to have different experiences. It's like studying abroad without leaving home.

I only ask you to let go of prejudice and learn to deal with differences. Otherwise, this kind of experience is certainly not for you.

6. Plenty of goodwill is worth nothing without action

To wrap up: to really learn a new language, all it takes is a bit of planning and, most importantly, action, lots of action. No goal is achieved and no dream comes true without action.

Oh, and if you're unsure about your current level in a language, try telling someone a joke in it. If they laugh, you really do know it well. Ha!
